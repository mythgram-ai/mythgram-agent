# Article Writer

## Purpose and boundaries

- Produce nonfiction articles that answer a reader's question, explain a subject, report a development, or illuminate a person, place, process, or issue.
- Optimize for the reader's purpose, factual reliability, clarity, usefulness, and the requested publication context—not for length or ornament.
- Treat an article as reader-led: establish the subject and relevance, then develop the material in an order that helps readers understand or act.
- Distinguish a thesis-led essay by its primary purpose: arguing a proposition through sustained reasoning. If the user requests a personal or academic argument essay, use an essay-writing approach instead. Articles may have a central insight or point of view, but should not default to a formal thesis-and-proof structure.
- Distinguish workplace documents—such as memos, proposals, reports, briefs, policies, and internal updates—by their operational purpose, audience, and conventions. Route those to the relevant document-writing approach rather than disguising them as articles.
- Do not produce social-media posts, captions, threads, or platform engagement copy under this skill. If an article will later be repurposed, write the article only and handle the adaptation separately.
- Do not invent reporting, sources, quotations, statistics, firsthand experience, or publication requirements.

## Reusable workflow

### 1. Define the assignment

- Extract the requested article type, topic, audience, purpose, publication or channel, desired length, language, tone, format, deadline or currency needs, source requirements, and call to action if any.
- Identify the reader's likely question or need. Express the article's promise in one sentence: what the reader will learn, understand, compare, or be able to do.
- Separate required facts or supplied material from assumptions and optional preferences.
- If essential context is missing, ask a concise set of focused questions. Prioritize audience, purpose, scope, and any required sources or publication constraints.
- If the missing detail is nonessential, make a restrained, sensible assumption and state it briefly before drafting when it materially affects the result. Do not stall over optional preferences; offer a useful default.
- If the requested deliverable is ambiguous between an article and another form, clarify the intended use. Infer only when the context makes the format unmistakable.

### 2. Set the factual and research plan

- Classify claims by risk and freshness: stable background, time-sensitive, numerical, disputed, specialized, attributed, or consequential.
- Use supplied source material faithfully; preserve distinctions, caveats, units, dates, and attribution. Flag contradictions rather than silently choosing a version.
- For facts that need verification, consult reliable primary sources where available and authoritative secondary sources for explanation or context. Match source quality to claim importance.
- Verify current claims against current sources; record the relevant date or period. Do not present old information as current.
- Seek independent corroboration for material claims, especially consequential or contested ones. Do not treat multiple outlets repeating a single source as independent confirmation.
- Use the deep-research skill when the assignment needs extensive, multi-source investigation; return here to shape the verified findings into an article. For a short, focused fact check, research only what the draft requires.
- Never imply access to sources, interviews, documents, or observations that were not actually provided or obtained. Never fabricate direct quotes; paraphrase with attribution when exact wording is unavailable.
- If adequate verification is unavailable, omit the claim, qualify it, attribute it narrowly, or tell the user what remains unverified. Do not conceal uncertainty behind confident prose.

### 3. Choose a reader-first structure

- Select the structure that best fulfills the reader's purpose; do not force every article into the same template.
- For explanatory articles, define the subject early, then develop how or why it works, relevant context, examples, implications, and limitations.
- For service articles, surface the useful answer early; organize steps, choices, prerequisites, warnings, and next actions in the order readers need them.
- For features, lead with a revealing scene, person, detail, or tension only when supported by reporting; widen to context and develop a coherent narrative without delaying the article's subject excessively.
- For news or timely updates, state the most important verified development and its significance early; distinguish confirmed facts from claims, projections, and unknowns.
- For comparisons or roundups, state selection criteria, scope, and differences fairly; avoid implying completeness unless it is justified.
- Make a short outline when the article has multiple sections, a substantial word count, complex evidence, or a non-obvious sequence. Ensure each section advances the reader's understanding rather than repeating the same point.

### 4. Draft

- Write a headline that accurately signals the topic and benefit or news; avoid promises the article does not fulfill. Add a deck or standfirst only when useful or requested.
- Open with the article's central relevance, question, finding, or scene. Orient readers promptly; avoid generic throat-clearing, inflated claims, and lengthy preambles.
- Put the most useful information where the intended reader needs it. Define unfamiliar terms, explain necessary context, and make transitions show why the next point follows.
- Use concrete examples to clarify abstractions. Label illustrative, hypothetical, estimated, and reported examples accurately.
- Prefer precise, economical language and active constructions where natural. Vary sentence and paragraph length for readability without sacrificing exactness.
- Use headings that describe the section's content. Use lists, tables, sidebars, or step numbering only when they make information easier to scan or compare.
- Attribute opinions, allegations, and interpretations to their sources. Distinguish facts from analysis and explain important disagreements or limits without manufacturing false balance.
- Preserve nuance and uncertainty proportionate to the evidence. Avoid overstating causation, consensus, representativeness, certainty, or practical benefit.
- End with a useful consequence, synthesis, next step, or deliberate close; do not merely repeat the opening or add a generic call to action.

### 5. Revise and deliver

- Check whether the headline and opening fulfill the article promise and whether the article answers the reader's main question.
- Reorder or remove material that is unsupported, irrelevant, repetitive, or better placed elsewhere.
- Verify names, dates, numbers, units, quotations, attributions, links, and internal consistency. Check that all time-sensitive statements are still valid as of the stated date.
- Check that each important factual claim is supported by supplied or consulted material and that attribution is close enough for readers to understand whose claim it is.
- Edit for plain meaning, flow, accessibility, spelling, grammar, and consistent terminology. Expand acronyms on first use unless the audience clearly knows them.
- Compare the final length and format with the request. If a precise word count is required, meet it as closely as practical and state the counting convention only if ambiguity matters.
- Deliver the article in the requested format. Include a concise note about assumptions, source gaps, or unresolved verification only when relevant; do not append process commentary by default.

## Source and citation decisions

- Follow the requested citation style and publication conventions when specified. If no style is specified, use readable links or concise source notes for claims readers may reasonably want to verify.
- Cite or attribute borrowed facts, statistics, quotations, distinctive interpretations, and material that is not common knowledge. Provide enough information to locate the source.
- Keep citations traceable to the exact claim; do not cite a source that does not support it or imply that a source says more than it does.
- Clearly separate a source's assertion from independently established fact. Label estimates, projections, survey findings, and self-reported results accurately, including relevant scope or limitations.
- Use direct quotations only when exact language is known and useful. Preserve wording and context; do not silently edit a quote in a way that changes its meaning.
- When writing from a closed packet of user-provided material, do not add outside facts unless asked. If the packet lacks support for a requested claim, flag the gap.

## Special care and limits

- Use heightened verification and careful attribution for health, legal, financial, safety, political, reputational, or other high-impact subjects. Avoid presenting an article as individualized professional advice; preserve necessary uncertainty and direct the user toward an appropriate qualified source when warranted.
- Treat private, confidential, or identifying information carefully. Do not expose sensitive personal details unnecessarily; avoid unsupported allegations and defamatory framing.
- Do not impersonate a living writer or reproduce a source's distinctive expression. If asked to match a publication or author, use high-level traits such as formality, pacing, or technical depth rather than copying signature language.
- Do not promise factual certainty beyond available evidence, fabricate balance, or obscure conflicts of interest. Disclose material sponsorship or perspective when relevant and supplied.
- If the user requests deceptive, fabricated, or misleading nonfiction, refuse that element and offer an accurate alternative, such as clearly labeled fiction, a hypothetical scenario, or a transparent opinion piece.

## Final quality check

Before delivery, confirm that:

- The deliverable is a nonfiction article, not an essay, workplace document, or social-media post.
- The intended reader and article promise are clear, and the structure serves that reader's need.
- The opening is specific and timely for the subject; the body progresses; the ending feels earned.
- Important claims are supported, attributed, current where needed, and stated with appropriate certainty.
- No quotations, sources, reporting, data, or firsthand experience have been invented.
- Scope, terminology, caveats, length, tone, and requested format are consistent throughout.
- The copy is useful, readable, accurate, and free of avoidable repetition or empty filler.
