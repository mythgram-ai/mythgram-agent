# Story Writer

## Purpose and boundaries

- Treat the work as fiction: invent events, characters, settings, and dialogue as requested, while distinguishing any real-world facts the user specifically asks to verify.
- Use this skill for story premises, outlines, character and world development, scene drafting, dialogue, stylistic revision, structural critique, and continuation of supplied fiction.
- Do not turn factual article, essay, report, or research requests into fiction; route those requests to the appropriate writing or research workflow.
- Honor the user's stated genre, audience, length, format, point of view, tense, tone, content limits, and canon. Ask before making a consequential change that conflicts with them.
- Do not imitate a living author's distinctive style. When asked, identify high-level traits (for example, clipped sentences, dry humor, close third person, or lush sensory detail) and write with that trait combination without claiming to reproduce the author.
- Avoid presenting invented real-world claims as verified facts. If a fictional work incorporates real settings or events, separate invented elements from factual claims when accuracy matters.

## Reusable workflow

1. **Classify the request.** Determine whether the user wants ideation, planning, drafting, revision, continuation, or critique. Identify the requested deliverable and whether the supplied text is source material to preserve.
2. **Extract constraints.** Record genre, premise, intended readership, length, POV, tense, voice, setting, characters, themes, ending requirements, content boundaries, and any no-go elements. Treat explicit user constraints as binding unless they conflict with safety or feasibility.
3. **Check available context.** Review the conversation and supplied documents for established facts, prior drafts, and decisions. Build a compact continuity ledger for ongoing work: names and descriptions, relationships, timeline, locations, rules, unresolved threads, and style choices.
4. **Resolve missing context proportionately.** Ask a focused question only when an unknown would materially change the result or make compliance uncertain. Otherwise choose a reasonable default, state it briefly if useful, and proceed. When the user asks for surprise or open-ended ideation, do not stall with a questionnaire.
5. **Choose the smallest useful planning depth.** Draft immediately for a clear short scene or story. For larger or structurally complex work, propose or create a premise, conflict, character goals, turning points, and ending before drafting. Match planning detail to the user's request; do not force an outline when they ask for finished prose.
6. **Create or revise toward a dramatic purpose.** Give each scene a viewpoint, immediate objective, obstacle, change, and consequence. Make actions and choices reveal character; use exposition only where it improves comprehension, tension, or emotional meaning.
7. **Check continuity and constraints.** Compare the draft against the continuity ledger and the user's requested form, length, tone, and boundaries. Resolve contradictions deliberately rather than silently introducing them.
8. **Deliver the requested artifact.** Put the story, scene, outline, critique, or revision first. Separate brief notes or assumptions from the fiction, and include them only when useful. Offer a concise account of substantial changes when revising, without burying the revised text.

## Decision points for missing context

- **Premise absent:** Offer a few distinct premises or invent one that follows the user's prompt. If the user asks to write a story without specifying a topic, pick a compelling, broadly accessible premise and proceed unless the intended genre or audience is critical.
- **Length absent:** Match the scale implied by the request. Use a compact scene for a scene request and a concise complete story for a story request; do not claim an arbitrary word count. Ask only if length materially affects the deliverable.
- **Genre, audience, or tone absent:** Infer from the prompt and context. Avoid extreme content or narrow genre conventions without a cue; make any important assumption visible in a short note.
- **POV or tense absent:** Choose the one that best serves the scene and remain consistent. For continuation, preserve the supplied work's choices.
- **Ending unspecified:** Aim for an emotionally and causally earned resolution appropriate to the setup. Leave a deliberate open ending only when requested or when ambiguity is clearly part of the intended effect.
- **Canon or continuity unclear:** Preserve what is explicit. Do not invent irreversible backstory, relationships, or world rules if a quick question can prevent a major mismatch. For low-impact gaps, make the least disruptive choice.
- **Revision brief vague:** Preserve the author's intent and strongest material. Improve clarity, momentum, characterization, and line-level expression conservatively; ask whether they want developmental, stylistic, or copy-level editing only when those approaches would lead to meaningfully different work.
- **Continuation point unclear:** Continue from the most recent supplied passage, preserving its scene state and voice. If multiple versions or points are present, ask which one is canonical.

## Craft guidance

### Premise, plot, and structure

- Make the premise express a protagonist, a desire, an obstacle, and a source of pressure or change. Prefer a specific, story-generating tension over a broad topic.
- Build causality: let consequential choices produce complications, revelations, reversals, and costs. Avoid a chain of unrelated incidents or convenient resolutions.
- Give the protagonist agency even when circumstances constrain them. Make the central conflict test a goal, belief, relationship, or vulnerability.
- Foreshadow important turns fairly. Seed information before relying on it, and let reveals alter the reader's understanding rather than merely add trivia.
- Make the ending answer the story's central dramatic question or deliberately reframe it. Earn resolution through prior choices and consequences; do not solve the main conflict through an unprepared coincidence.
- For a short story, focus on one principal change. For a novel or serial plan, track the central arc and subplots without resolving every thread too early.

### Characters and setting

- Differentiate characters through goals, values, knowledge, speech habits, decisions, and relationships, not just physical descriptions.
- Give important characters credible motives and limits. Let antagonists pursue intelligible aims rather than exist solely to obstruct.
- Reveal traits through behavior under pressure, selective detail, and interaction. Avoid explaining a character's entire history before it matters.
- Make setting affect action, choices, mood, or stakes. Establish only the world rules needed for the current scene, and apply them consistently.
- Track what each character knows, believes, wants, and can do at each point in the timeline.

### Scenes, dialogue, voice, and pacing

- Enter scenes near the point of friction and leave after a meaningful shift, unless a slower passage has a clear atmospheric or emotional purpose.
- Anchor readers in who is present, where they are, and what is happening; orient without interrupting momentum.
- Use concrete sensory details selectively. Prefer details that establish viewpoint, setting, mood, or action over decorative inventory.
- Give dialogue a purpose: pursue, evade, test, persuade, conceal, connect, or change the balance. Use subtext where characters have reason not to say exactly what they mean.
- Keep dialogue distinct and readable. Use action beats and attribution where needed for clarity; avoid repetitive tags, excessive adverbs, and long speeches that do not change the scene.
- Match sentence rhythm, diction, and imagery to viewpoint, genre, and emotional temperature. Maintain a coherent voice without making every sentence ornate or every character sound alike.
- Vary scene length and intensity to control pace. Slow down for consequential choices and emotional turns; compress routine transitions and repeated information.
- Keep viewpoint knowledge bounded. Do not reveal thoughts, facts, or future events unavailable to the chosen narrator unless the form deliberately permits it.

## Revision and continuation

- Identify the requested level: developmental (structure and stakes), scene-level (purpose and progression), line-level (clarity, rhythm, voice), or proofread (mechanics). If not specified, address the most evident issue while preserving the author's core intent.
- Preserve effective phrasing, intentional ambiguity, and distinctive voice. Do not rewrite wholesale when targeted edits will work.
- For a requested rewrite, provide the revised passage in full when practical. For critique, cite concrete moments from the supplied text and pair each major issue with an actionable option.
- For continuation, read enough prior text to establish voice, POV, tense, scene geography, character knowledge, and active conflicts. Continue from the established moment rather than recap or restart.
- Do not silently retcon established events. If a requested change creates a contradiction, explain the conflict briefly and offer the least disruptive fix or ask which version to follow.
- When a user supplies a style sample of their own work, use it to preserve continuity. Keep any analysis of style descriptive and practical rather than turning it into a rigid imitation checklist.

## Output and quality checks

Before responding, verify that:

- The output is the requested kind of fiction or fiction support, not an unrelated article, essay, or explanation.
- The central action, viewpoint, and scene transitions are understandable without unnecessary exposition.
- Character motivations and choices are plausible within the established story, and dialogue reflects the speakers and situation.
- Tension, pacing, and emotional turns serve the intended genre and scale.
- The ending or handoff follows from the setup and leaves no accidental dangling central conflict.
- Names, timeline, setting, knowledge, rules, and prior events remain consistent with the supplied canon.
- The user's format, requested length, content boundaries, and explicit constraints are respected.
- Any assumptions, optional alternatives, or revision notes are brief and clearly separated from the fictional text.

If a check fails, revise before delivering. Prefer a polished, complete response over a long explanation of the process.
